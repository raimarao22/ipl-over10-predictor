The question

Halfway through a T20 innings, broadcasters often project the final score by doubling the current total. We ask whether a model can do better, and which information beyond the score actually helps.

One row
One IPL first innings, frozen at the end of over 10
Target
The final first-innings total, in runs
Prediction time
Immediately after the last ball of over 10
Known at that moment
Every ball of overs 1–10, the venue, the season, and results of earlier matches

What we do not claim. The model covers IPL men's first innings scheduled for 20 overs. It predicts; it does not show what causes scores. It is not a betting tool.

The data

Ball-by-ball records for every IPL match from April 2008 to May 2026, from Cricsheet (CC-BY-4.0). Each match file lists every delivery: runs, extras, wickets and the over it was bowled in.

From 1,243 matches to 1,211 innings

Natural all-outs after over 10 stay in, so collapses remain in the data. Second innings are left out entirely: a chase stops once the target is reached, so its total measures something different.

Scoring jumped from 2023

Development seasons only (2008–2024). Average totals rose from 163 in 2008–2022 to 187 in 2023–2024, the first two seasons of the Impact Player rule. The rise coincides with the rule; we do not claim the rule caused it.

Compare the two scoring eras

Development seasons only. The faded bars show the other era for comparison. Both halves of the innings got higher-scoring, which is why we tested an era indicator. It turned out to be the one extra feature with an established gain (see Results).

Quality checks

  • Season labels such as “2007/08” are inconsistent, so the season comes from the match date.
  • The same ground appears under several names (for example Feroz Shah Kotla and Arun Jaitley Stadium), so venue names are standardised.
  • “Retired hurt” is not counted as a wicket.
  • Cricsheet numbers overs from 0. An independent over-by-over recount of random innings matched our values exactly.
  • Some seasons were played outside India: 2009 in South Africa, all of 2020 and parts of 2014 and 2021 in the UAE. The venue average we tested gives each ground its own history, so these seasons do not distort it (venue was not kept in the final model).

Preprocessing

Each innings is cut off at the end of over 10. The model predicts the runs still to come (final total minus the over-10 score), and we add the over-10 score back, so every error is reported on the final total.

No peeking at the future

  • Venue and league averages for a match use only matches played on earlier dates.
  • The first 11 innings of 2008 have too little history for these averages, so they are used only as history, never for training.
  • Scaling is fitted on training seasons only, inside the model pipeline.
  • The 2025–2026 seasons are held back for one final test and were not used for any decision.

Why doubling falls short

Both rules draw a straight line from the over-10 score to the final total, and both force it through zero: a team on 0 is predicted to finish on 0. Try moving the line yourself. Each dot is one 2019–2024 innings.

22.0runs average miss for your line

Our model

Ridge regression: a straight-line formula fitted to minimise squared error, with a small penalty that keeps the weights from growing too large. It is easy to interpret and small enough to run in your browser, which is how the calculator on the home tab works.

runs still to come = 94.5 + 0.11 × runs at over 10 − 5.7 × wickets + 8.8 × Impact era

Read it like this: start from 94.5 runs still to come, add 0.11 for every run already scored, take off about 6 for every wicket down, and add about 9 in the Impact Player era. For an average innings that comes to about 91 runs after over 10. The runs already scored only weakly predict how many are still to come.

Average runs in overs 11–20 by wickets lost at over 10, development seasons. Teams with five or more down add about 40 fewer runs than teams with none.

Checking the straight-line assumption

If a straight-line model fits well, its errors should scatter evenly around zero, with no curve, at every level of prediction. Each dot is one 2019–2024 innings; the line is the average error in each band of predictions.

We also tried gradient boosting and random forests, which can capture interactions between features. Gradient boosting was 0.26 runs better on average, but the 95% confidence interval for that gap (−0.71 to +0.18) includes zero, so we kept the simpler ridge model.

Evaluation design

We never let the model learn from the future, using walk-forward validation. To test a season, we train only on the seasons before it, then move forward one season and repeat. Every model choice was made this way, on the 2019–2024 seasons.

How we score predictions

  • Average error (MAE)How many runs off a prediction is, on average. Our main measure.
  • Signed errorPrediction minus actual, averaged. Negative means the method predicts too low.
  • Confidence intervalsWe resample innings 5,000 times to check whether a gap between two methods could be chance.
  • 90% rangeBuilt from the model's past errors; we check how often real totals land inside it.

    Labels: late collapse means 5 or more wickets fell after over 10 and the model was 30+ runs too high; late explosion means 75+ runs came in the last 5 overs and the model was 30+ runs too low; the third label marks innings every method missed by 35 runs or more.

    The largest misses tend to be late collapses, which no method can see at over 10, and late explosions, which push totals well past every projection. The 90% range is lopsided for the same reason: big totals overshoot more often than small ones fall short.

    Limitations

    • Era shift Only two Impact Player seasons were available for training, and the model still underpredicts 2023–2024 by about 7 runs on average.
    • No player information The model does not know who is batting or who is left to bowl.
    • Straight-line wicket effect The model takes off the same 5.7 runs for every wicket and cannot know an innings ends at 10 down. Most of the data is 0–5 wickets at over 10, so the predictor stops at 7 and flags 6–7 as rare.
    • Modest gains beyond the score Everything we added on top of the over-10 score improved the error by about 0.4 runs, and that total is not statistically established. Only the era indicator’s share of it is, and that gain is almost all in 2023–2024.
    • Slightly optimistic validation Choices were made on the same seasons we report, so the final test gives the fairer number.
    • Small test set 141 innings in 2025–2026, so small differences there may be chance.
    • Scope Rain-reduced matches and second innings are excluded, so results do not apply to them.

    Conclusion

    At the halfway point, a simple model that knows the score, the wickets lost and the era misses by 16.8 runs on average, against 22.0 for the commentator's doubling rule: about a quarter less error.

    What mattered

    Modelling the score properly: a straight line with its own starting point gives most of the gain over both rules. The Impact Player era indicator adds the only extra gain we could establish, about 0.35 runs, nearly all in 2023–2024.

    What did not

    Recent momentum, the venue and gradient boosting each changed the error by less than 0.3 runs, which we could not tell apart from chance. Wickets clearly lower the runs still to come (about 6 runs each in the model), but adding them did not measurably improve accuracy.

    Still to come

    These are validation results. The conclusion is provisional until the one-time test on 2025–2026.

    What we would try next

    • Player information, such as who is batting and how many overs the best bowlers have left.
    • Retraining as more Impact Player seasons arrive, which may reduce the underprediction of high-scoring innings.
    • A separate model for chases, which behave differently because the target shapes how teams bat.
